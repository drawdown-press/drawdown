From Measurement to Correction: Using i1 Spot Color Compare with Drawdown

X-Rite's Spot Color Compare is a great tool for reading how far off your print is from the target. What it doesn't tell you is what to do about it. Here's how to use it properly — and then how to close the gap with Drawdown.

What Spot Color Compare actually does

Inside i1Profiler, under the Tools section, there's a workflow called Spot Color Compare. It's designed to do one job well: measure two color patches with your i1 Pro 3 (or i1 Pro 3 Plus), calculate the ΔE between them, and show you the drift. Reference on the left, Sample on the right, ΔE in the middle.

It's a genuinely useful piece of software, but it stops where the interesting question begins. Once you know the ΔE, you still have to figure out which inks to move to close it. That's the handoff to Drawdown. Here's the full workflow, top to bottom.

Part 1: Running the Spot Comparison

Step 1
Warm up and calibrate the i1

Plug the i1 into your computer. Give it three to five minutes to stabilize — spectros drift when cold, and a rushed measurement will send you chasing ghosts. Calibrate against the white tile when i1Profiler prompts you.

Step 2
Open Spot Color Compare

From the Workflow Selector, go to Tools → Spot Color Compare. You'll see two large color panels labeled Reference Color and Sample Color, and a Colorimetry panel on the right showing L*a*b* values and ΔE.

Step 3
Lock down the measurement settings

Before measuring anything, set your shop standard on the right-side controls: Illuminant D50, Observer 2°, Color space CIE Lab, ΔE type ΔE 2000. These are the professional defaults and they're what Drawdown expects.

For the measurement condition (top of each Spot Color panel), use M1 — the D50 setting with a controlled UV component. It's the industry standard for wide-format work because most modern sign substrates contain optical brighteners that react to UV, and M0 doesn't handle those consistently. Once you pick M1, use it every measurement. Mixing conditions across a session produces garbage ΔE.

Step 4
Measure the Reference (your target)

The Reference is the color you're chasing — the proof, the client's swatch, the Pantone chip. Lay the i1 on it and press the button. The Reference L*a*b* fills in. That's your target color, measured, in real numbers.

Step 5
Measure the Sample (what's on press or printer)

Pull a sheet off the press or printer and measure the same color area. The Sample L*a*b* fills in, and Spot Color Compare immediately shows the ΔE between them. That number is how far off you are.

Step 6
Measure the substrate too

One extra reading worth taking: measure a bare, unprinted area of your material. Note the L*a*b*. That's your material white, and it's the number most color-matching guides forget matters — but it's often the reason a build won't hit the target no matter what you do. Save it.

Reading the ΔE — what the number means

Here's where a lot of shops get stuck: Spot Color Compare hands you a number, but never tells you whether it's good or bad. In practice, ΔE reads on a ladder that pressmen learn by experience — or by having someone put it in plain terms:

THE ΔE LADDER (ΔE 2000)

  • < 1.0You're inside a drawdown match. Don't chase it.
  • 1.0 – 2.0Commercial match. Most jobs pass here.
  • 2.0 – 3.5Visible side-by-side. Correct if the client will see both.
  • > 3.5The client will call. Correct before you run the job.

Where you set your pass line depends on the job. Critical brand color work often demands ΔE under 2. General commercial work is fine at 2–3. If your Spot Color Compare reading is 4, 5, or higher, you have a correction to make — and that's where you leave i1Profiler behind and pick up Drawdown.

Part 2: Handing off to Drawdown

Now you have three real measurements sitting in front of you: the Reference Lab (target), the Sample Lab (on-press or on-printer), and the material Lab. Spot Color Compare has done its job — it's told you the gap. Drawdown's job is to close it.

Step 7
Open Drawdown Pro in your browser

Head to drawdown.press. In Pro, all three input panels (Target, On press/printer, and Material) accept Lab values directly — no need to convert anything by hand.

Step 8
Enter the three measurements

Switch each panel to Lab mode and enter the values from Spot Color Compare:

  • Target: the Reference L*a*b* from Spot Color Compare.
  • On press/printer: the Sample L*a*b*.
  • Material: the bare-substrate L*a*b* you measured in step 6.

Also enter the CMYK build that's currently running in the small "Build running" row — Drawdown needs to know your starting recipe to tell you what to move.

Step 9
Read the correction and apply it

Drawdown returns the CMYK moves — +2.3 C, −1.5 M, ±0 Y, +0.8 K, or whatever the math says. Apply those exact moves to your build in the RIP, print a new sample, and measure it again with Spot Color Compare. The ΔE should drop into the acceptable range.

If Drawdown flags a substrate ceiling — the warning that says "this is the closest match on this material" — you've hit the physical limit of what this stock can produce, and no further correction will get you closer. That's honest information; change the media or accept the match.

Why this workflow works

The reason this pairing is powerful is that each tool does exactly what it's best at. Spot Color Compare is a measurement instrument's software — it reads reality precisely and reports it. Drawdown is a correction engine — it takes the reported reality and tells you what to change. Neither replaces the other, and neither works as well alone.

A lot of shops get halfway through this workflow — they measure faithfully, they know the ΔE, they read the color drift — but they still guess at the fix. That's where hours vanish and remakes pile up. Drawdown finishes the workflow: measurement to number, number to correction, correction to a sheet that matches.
